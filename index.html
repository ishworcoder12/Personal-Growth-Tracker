ACT toolkit

Guided ladder

▾

100 rungs in 10 tiers, easiest first. Finish every rung in a tier to unlock the next. Repeat a rung until your worry drops, and skip your usual escapes while you do it.

Your ladder

Situations you're avoiding, roughly ordered from easiest to hardest. Climb one rung at a time — you don't need to be ready, just willing.

Spotlight experiment

"Everyone is watching" is a prediction, not a fact. Run the test instead of trusting the feeling.

1. Predict. Before the rep, write exactly what you think people will notice and judge. Be specific.

2. Point outward. During it, put your attention on them, not on yourself. Find three things about the other person: clothes, voice, what they're doing.

3. Compare. Afterwards, write what really happened next to your prediction, without softening it. A wrong prediction is data. One that has been avoided is just a fear story.

Reverse test. Next time you're in a shop or on the road, try to remember the strangers you passed. You'll recall almost none, and they won't remember you either.

Patterns to notice

▾

Not for daily use. Open one the moment you catch yourself. Each of these is a move you make, not who you are, and a move can change today.

Learning about it instead of doing it

Reading, studying and building feel like progress, and past a point they aren't. You already know enough to do a rep. More insight changes nothing; repetition does.

Counter-move: Before any new material, ask whether you've done a rep today. If not, do the smallest one first, within the hour.

Waiting to feel ready

The feeling doesn't come first. Confidence shows up after you act scared and nothing terrible happens. Waiting keeps the fear in charge while the days pass.

Counter-move: Start while still nervous, on purpose. Feeling unready is the cue to go. Do it in the next ten minutes, before you finish arguing with yourself.

Saving the change for a future reset

Studying abroad and a better career are good goals, but neither will change a reflex. Reflexes change through reps, and a new city is just a new place to avoid things. If you aren't practicing today, the future can't be different yet.

Counter-move: Do today's rep in today's life. Your future self is built from these ones.

Talking about it instead of doing it

Putting your struggles into words, here or with a chatbot, is safe: nobody judges you and nothing is risked. That's exactly why it can replace the practice. A conversation about the fear can become a hiding place.

Counter-move: Do the real-world rep first. Keep any chat for planning or reviewing an actual attempt, and aim to need it less each week.

Using meditation or philosophy as a retreat

A sit or a concept is either preparation for a rep or a hiding place, and only one of those is practice. If it replaces the rep, it's avoidance with better branding.

Counter-move: Sit if it helps, then go do the rep that same day.

Numbing right after the spike

Games or the phone the moment anxiety peaks bring relief, and that relief is what makes it a trap. It teaches your body the fear was justified and blocks the habituation that would shrink it with time.

Counter-move: After a spike, stay with it for ten more minutes before reaching for anything. Phone down, nothing else.

Swinging between 'special' and 'hopeless'

Both stories excuse you from the work. "Special" says the rules don't apply to you; "hopeless" says there's no point trying. Underneath both is a plain fact: you're an ordinary person out in an ordinary life, and practice still works.

Counter-move: Name the story when it shows up ("that's the special story"), then do the rep anyway.

Having few chances to practice

Your work gives you very little daily contact with people. That's a real constraint, not a flaw. But practice needs people, so contact has to be planned, and a plan that isn't scheduled is just a hope.

Counter-move: This week, put one recurring contact outside work on the calendar: a class, a club, a call, a group. Weigh it in future job and place decisions.

A slip is information, not a verdict. Do the next rep.

Traditional Vipassana — beginner to advanced

Foundation

  • Sīla first. In the classical framework, ethical conduct (the five precepts, or whatever restraint you hold to) isn't separate from the meditation — a mind still agitated by unskillful action is a weak place to practice.
  • Posture and time, not intensity. Spine upright but not rigid, hands resting, eyes closed. Start with short sits (10–20 min) and let duration grow on its own — force here just creates tension.

Beginner: Ānāpāna (breath awareness)

  • Attention rests on the natural breath at the nostrils/upper lip — not the belly yet, just the air's contact. You're not lengthening, slowing, or deepening it; you're observing what's there.
  • This stage is samatha — sharpening and steadying attention. It isn't insight practice yet; it's building the instrument insight will later use.
  • When the mind wanders (it will, constantly), simply note "wandered" or "thinking" and return to the breath — no self-criticism. The returning is the practice, not a failure.
  • If the chest tightens or the breath feels hard to find: you've likely shifted from observing to steering it. Widen attention from the nostrils to the whole chest/belly rising and falling until it feels natural again.
  • Physical discomfort (an itch, an ache): the beginner instruction is to hold still and just observe the sensation for a while before adjusting — this is the first taste of not reacting immediately.

Intermediate: body scanning and vedanā

  • Systematic body scan. Once attention is reasonably steady on the breath, it moves through the body region by region — scalp, face, neck, shoulders, down each arm, torso, each leg, each foot.
  • Gross to subtle. Early scans mostly find gross sensation — pressure, ache, itch, numbness, or blank areas with nothing noticeable. With repetition, subtler sensation (tingling, vibration, warmth, coolness) starts to emerge.
  • Noting/labeling (a parallel Theravada method, associated with the Mahasi Sayadaw tradition): silently label each moment as it's experienced — "rising, falling" on the breath, "heaviness" or "pulling" in a body area, "thinking" when the mind wanders.
  • Equanimity toward all three — pleasant, unpleasant, and neutral sensation get the exact same quality of attention. The actual skill being trained is non-reaction (not chasing or defending).
  • Walking meditation (cankama) is traditionally alternated with sitting — slow, deliberate walking, attention on the sensations of lifting/moving/placing the foot. It keeps momentum and integrates mindfulness into daily motion.

Advanced: free-flow and the progress of insight

  • Free-flow sweep. Once individual body parts are clear, attention moves as one continuous wave from head to feet (and back), feeling sensation across the whole body at once rather than in isolated points.
  • Subtle vibration. Sustained practice at this level often reveals sensation as much finer and more rapidly arising-and-passing than the gross sensations noticed early on — something like vibration, current, or pulse-like changes.
  • Recognizable stages (progress of insight). Classical Theravada maps (e.g. the Visuddhimagga's ñāṇas) describe a fairly consistent sequence long-term practitioners trend through — more clarity, more equanimity, less identification with passing states.
  • The three characteristics — impermanence (anicca), unsatisfactoriness (dukkha), non-self (anattā) — stop being ideas at this stage and become experiential features of experience itself.
  • Integration. The point was never the cushion in isolation — the same non-reactive, moment-to-moment awareness gets carried into eating, walking, speaking, conflict. Formal sit is just a training ground.

This ladder is traditionally taught progressively, under a teacher, especially past the intermediate stage — the deeper stages can surface real discomfort (fear, grief, agitation), which is data, not a signal to stop entirely.
